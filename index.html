How a real combine harvester works

A combine harvester is called a combine because it combines several farm jobs into one machine. Driving across a wheat field, it does five things in a row:

1 · Reel

A big slowly turning paddle wheel at the front. It gently pushes the stalks back onto the knife so they fall the right way.

2 · Cutter bar

A long row of pointed guards with a knife sliding back and forth over them. Every stalk gets snipped like scissors, about 1,000 times a minute.

3 · Feeder

An auger and a conveyor carry the cut crop back and up into the machine.

4 · Threshing

A spinning drum knocks the grain out of the ears; sieves and a fan blow the chaff away. We skip this — cress leaves are the bit we want.

5 · Grain tank

Clean grain collects in a tank on top, and a long pipe (the unloading auger) empties it into a trailer.

And the cress combine?

Reel, cutter bar, a ramp instead of the feeder, and a tank. Four of the five jobs, at about one-fortieth of the size.

Meet the Cress Combine

How it works in one sentence

You push it along the rails holding the button; the wheels turn the reel through a rubber-band belt; the reel sweeps cress onto a knife that snips back and forth ten times a second; and the cut cress is pushed up a ramp into the tank.

Reel

Four card paddles on a skewer. Driven by the wheels, so it only turns when the machine moves.

Cutter bar

A plastic guard comb (fixed) with a plastic knife (sliding) on top — ten teeth, 10 mm apart.

N20 motor + crank

A tiny geared motor turns a crank; a lolly-stick arm turns the spinning into sliding.

Ramp & tank

A gentle card ramp behind the knife leads into a dustpan-shaped plastic tub.

Battery & button

4 × AA box on top; fuse, ON/OFF switch and a hold-to-run button on the handle.

Chassis & rails

Foam-board body, skewer axles in straw bearings, bottle-cap wheels on 10 mm wooden rails.

Honest note

This is a design to build, not a kit that has been tested a hundred times. Every size here is a sensible starting point, and the troubleshooting page says what to change if something doesn’t work first time. That’s normal — it’s what engineers do.

05

The cutter: an N20 motor and a pair of scissors

An N20 gear motor is a tiny electric motor joined to a gearbox. The whole thing is about 12 mm across and 25 mm long — smaller than a sugar cube and a half — and weighs about 10 g. The gearbox trades speed for strength: the motor inside spins thousands of times a minute, but the output shaft turns slowly and strongly. We use the 6 V, 300 RPM version: five turns a second.

Turning spin into slide

A pin on a spinning disc goes round in a circle. Join a stick to the pin and the other end moves back and forth — exactly how a steam locomotive’s connecting rods work, but backwards. On a combine this stick is called the pitman arm.

Why not a spinning blade?

A spinning blade has to go very fast to cut soft stems — think of a strimmer — and a fast blade is dangerous. A scissor action cuts soft cress slowly and gently, using plastic teeth. It is the safer design, and it’s also what real combines use.

Stroke = pitch

The pin is 5 mm from the centre, so the knife travels 10 mm each way — exactly the gap between two teeth. Every knife tooth sweeps across one whole gap on every half-turn, so no stem can hide between teeth.

06

How a scissor cut works

Scissors don’t cut because they are sharp — they cut because two edges slide past each other while pressed together. Anything caught between them is pinched and sheared. That’s why the knife and guard must lie flat against each other; if there’s a gap, a soft stem just folds into it.

The V funnels

Pointed teeth guide stems into the gaps as the machine moves forward, so each one is cut close to where the edges meet — the strongest part of the cut.

Flat and snug

The mount plate overhangs the knife and holds it flat on the guard. Tight enough not to lift; loose enough to slide with a light push.

Guards stick out further

Guard teeth are 15 mm long, knife teeth only 12 mm. The fixed guard always reaches further forward than the moving knife.

The numbers behind a clean cut

There are only three numbers to understand. The rest is just building carefully.

NumberSumWith our design
Cuts per second2 × (motor RPM ÷ 60) — one cut each way2 × (300 ÷ 60) = 10 cuts/s
Distance per cutpush speed ÷ cuts per second20 mm/s ÷ 10 = 2 mm
Reel speed ratioreel radius ÷ wheel radius25 ÷ 15 ≈ 1.7

The golden rule

Between two cuts the machine should move forward less than half a tooth gap (5 mm). Then every stem is caught by a V before the next stem arrives, and nothing gets flattened.

Distance per cutWhat happens
≤ 5 mmClean cut — every stem snipped
5–10 mmMostly cut — a few stems bend over
> 10 mmPushing too fast — stems get flattened, not cut
So how fast do I push?

With a 300 RPM motor the knife makes 10 cuts a second, so any push speed up to 50 mm a second keeps within the golden rule. A comfortable 20 mm a second — a 20 cm tray in 10 seconds — gives lots of margin.

Grow the cress first

Cress takes about a week, so plant it before building — it will be ready when the machine is.

  1. Choose the tray. No wider than 110 mm and about 20–30 mm deep, washed in hot soapy water. A cut-down food container works.
  2. Build the bed. Fold kitchen paper into a thick pad so its top is about 5 mm below the rim. Soak it, then pour away the extra water.
  3. Sow thickly and evenly. Sprinkle seeds so they nearly touch, leaving the first 20 mm at each end bare so the cutter can start and stop on empty ground.
  4. Days 0–2: dark and damp. Cover with a plate or put in a cupboard. Mist with a spray bottle if the paper looks pale.
  5. Days 3–7: light. Uncover on a bright windowsill. Turn the tray every day — cress leans toward the light, and straight stems cut more cleanly.
  6. Harvest at 50–60 mm tall, when the leaves are fully open and green.
Science bonus

Cress leaning toward the window is phototropism: the stem grows faster on its shady side, which bends it toward the light. Turning the tray every day keeps it straight — which our knife will like.

Build 1 — Rails, chassis and wheels

The field

  1. Place the tray in the middle of a board (an old chopping board or a piece of foam board) about 450 mm long.
  2. Glue a wooden rail on each side, tight against the tray walls, 120 mm apart centre to centre. The rail tops must be level with the tray rim; pack underneath with card if needed. grown-up: glue
  3. Leave a run-up. The rails should stick out about 250 mm past one end of the tray, so the machine can park off the crop.

The chassis

  1. Cut parts A to E from foam board using the templates. grown-up: craft knife
  2. Make the bearings. Push a Ø 5 mm hole through each side plate at the marks. Glue a 12 mm piece of straw into each hole. A skewer should spin in the straw freely without wobbling. grown-up: glue
  3. Glue the side plates under the base plate, flush with its two long edges, straw holes towards the bottom.
  4. Axles. Cut a 150 mm skewer for the back axle and a 170 mm one for the front. Slide them through the straws. The front axle sticks out 20 mm more on the belt side.
  5. Wheels. Pierce the exact centre of four bottle caps. Push them onto the axles, open side facing in, so the wheel centres are 120 mm apart, then glue. Wheels and axles turn together. grown-up: bradawl
  6. Rolling test. Put the chassis on the rails and give it a gentle push. It should roll 30 cm in a straight line without rubbing. If it wanders, one wheel is off-centre — re-glue it.
  7. Handle. Glue two overlapping lolly sticks to the back edge at about 40°, pointing up and back.
Train link

If the wheels keep slipping off the rails, glue a thin card disc slightly bigger than the cap to the inside of each wheel. That’s a flange — the same trick real trains use as a backup.

Build 2 — The cutter bar sandwich

This is the most important part of the machine. Take your time: evenly spaced teeth and a flat, snug knife matter more than anything else.

  1. Mark the teeth. On the plastic, draw the guard comb G (100 mm long, 16 mm base) and the knife H (112 mm long, 6 mm base). Mark a dot every 10 mm along each edge with a fine pen.
  2. Cut the teeth. Snip each V from both sides with strong scissors: guard teeth 15 mm long and 8 mm wide at the root; knife teeth 12 mm long and 9 mm wide. Sand off any whiskers so the edges are crisp and straight.
  3. Pin hole. Make a Ø 3 mm hole in the knife, 5 mm from the long end. grown-up: bradawl
  4. Spacer. Cut a 100 × 8 mm strip of the same plastic, add one layer of masking tape on top, and glue it along the back 8 mm of the guard base. The spacer is a hair thicker than the knife.
  5. Lay in the knife. Put the knife on the front half of the guard base, in front of the spacer, with its teeth lying over the guard teeth and the long end sticking out 12 mm on the motor side.
  6. Close the sandwich. Glue mount plate D on top of the spacer, with its front edge level with the front of the guard base. The long end of D, with the shaft hole, goes on the same side as the knife’s long end. The plate now overhangs the knife and holds it flat. Don’t let any glue reach the knife. grown-up: glue
  7. Slide test. Push the knife end with a finger. It should slide the full 10 mm with a light push, and must not lift or rattle.

Too loose?

If the knife lifts, glue two small hold-down strips of plastic under the front edge of the plate, one near each end, so they just touch the top of the knife.

Too stiff?

Rub a candle over both plastic faces — wax is food-safe and slippery. Check no glue has squeezed into the slot.

Build 3 — Motor, crank and pitman arm

  1. Make the crank. Glue three Ø 20 mm discs of plastic together (or use a flat 20 mm button). Make a snug centre hole for the 3 mm shaft, and a second hole exactly 5 mm from the centre. Measure twice — this sets the stroke. grown-up: bradawl
  2. Crank pin. Push a 12 mm piece of skewer into the off-centre hole and glue it square.
  3. Fit the motor. From the top, push the N20’s shaft down through the shaft hole in plate D until the gold gearbox sits flat. Glue the gearbox to the plate and add a cable tie around it. Keep glue off the shaft and the silver motor can. grown-up: glue
  4. Fit the crank onto the shaft underneath the plate, pin pointing down. Push it on hard, then add a small dab of glue on the flat side of the D-shaft. No glue near the gearbox.
  5. Pitman arm. Cut 45 mm of lolly stick with two Ø 3 mm holes 35 mm apart. Drop one hole over the crank pin, then glue a bead or a blob of glue onto the tip of the pin so the arm can’t jump off. Don’t glue the arm itself.
  6. Join the arm to the knife with a brass split pin through both holes. Open the legs gently so the joint swivels freely.
  7. Wiggle test. Push the knife slowly back and forth by hand. The arm should follow without catching on the plate. If the arm is lower than the knife, pack a small plastic washer under the joint.
Don’t force the motor

A tiny gearbox can strip if you twist the shaft hard by hand. If something feels stiff, find what is rubbing rather than forcing it. The first powered test comes after the wiring.

Build 4 — Header arms, reel and belt

Attach the header

  1. Glue the cutter unit (plate D) across the lower front ends of the two header arms C, so the arms are just outside the 90 mm cut width and the motor end hangs out beyond one arm. grown-up: glue
  2. Set the height with a gauge. Put the chassis on the rails over the tray. Stand a 15 mm block of wood on the cress bed and rest the knife on it. Hold each arm against its side plate and mark through its two pin holes (at 8 and 18 mm).
  3. Pin the arms. Pierce the marks and fit brass split pins through arm and side plate, both sides. Two pins per arm stop the header swinging.

Reel and belt

  1. Build the reel. Slide two card hub discs onto a 130 mm skewer, 88 mm apart. Glue four 85 × 15 mm paddles between them at 12, 3, 6 and 9 o’clock. The paddle tips should be 25 mm from the axle.
  2. Mount the reel through the front holes of both arms so the paddles pass about 5 mm above the knife teeth and a little in front of them. The skewer sticks out 15 mm on the belt side, opposite the motor.
  3. Make two equal pulleys. Wrap masking tape around the reel skewer end and the front-axle end until each is about 10 mm across. They must be the same size.
  4. Fit the belt. Stretch a small rubber band between the two pulleys, not crossed. Push the machine forward by hand: the bottom paddles must move backward, towards the knife. If they go the wrong way, cross the band into a figure of eight.
A built-in clutch

If the reel catches on something, the rubber band just slips. Nothing breaks, and the knife motor isn’t affected.

Build 5 — Wiring

The whole circuit is one loop: power leaves the battery on the red wire, passes through three safety parts and the motor, and comes back on the black wire.

  1. Batteries OUT of the box while wiring. Always.
  2. Capacitor. Connect the 100 nF “104” capacitor across the two motor terminals (or across the two motor wires, close to the motor). It stops the motor crackling on radios and TVs. Either way round is fine. grown-up if soldering
  3. Fuse first. Connect the battery red wire to one leg of the resettable fuse with a lever connector.
  4. Switch, then button. From the fuse, run red wire to the ON/OFF switch (skip if it’s built into the box), then to the hold-to-run button, then to the motor’s red wire.
  5. Black wire from the battery straight to the motor’s black wire.
  6. Tidy up. Hot-glue the button into a hole in the top of the handle. Tape the wires along the handle and chassis, and use cable ties to keep them well away from the crank.
  7. First spin. Lift the machine off the rails. Switch ON, then press the button for two seconds. The knife should blur side to side and stop the moment you let go. It doesn’t matter which way the motor turns.

1 · Fuse

If the knife jams, the motor draws more current. The PTC fuse warms up and switches itself off. It resets after cooling for a minute.

2 · ON/OFF switch

The master switch. OFF whenever the machine isn’t on the rails.

3 · Hold-to-run

Only runs while pressed. Let go and it stops — like a lawnmower’s safety bar.

Build 6 — Ramp, tank and first harvest

Ramp and tank

  1. Cut the tank. Cut the front wall of a small plastic tub down to 8 mm high, making a dustpan shape. grown-up: scissors or knife
  2. Fit the tank under the front of the base plate, just behind the cutter unit, with tape or a dab of glue. Its bottom must clear the cut stubble by at least 5 mm.
  3. Fit the ramp. Glue ramp E from the back edge of plate D up to the low front wall of the tank, at about 25°. A gentle slope matters: cress won’t climb a steep one.

First harvest

  1. Safety check with a grown-up, using the checklist on the safety plan.
  2. Practice run on a spare tray of long grass, or a few cress stems, before the real crop.
  3. Park the machine on the run-up, knife just before the crop. Switch ON.
  4. Hold the button and push slowly — about 20 mm a second, so a 20 cm tray takes 10 seconds. Keep one steady speed.
  5. At the far end, let go of the button, switch OFF, and take the batteries out before touching anything near the knife.
  6. Empty the tank into a sieve, rinse the cress under the cold tap, and pat it dry.
Egg & cress sandwiches (for 2)

Hard-boil 2 eggs (10 minutes), cool in cold water, peel and mash with a spoon of mayonnaise and a pinch of salt and pepper. Spread on buttered bread, scatter a big handful of freshly harvested cress on top, close, and cut into triangles. Farm to table in 15 minutes.

Upgrade 1 — Adjustable cutting height

Different crops need different heights. Small cress wants a low cut; a lumpy bed needs a higher one. Real combines let the driver raise and lower the header from the cab; ours uses three rows of pin holes.

  1. Mark the rows. On each side plate, around the two existing pin holes, mark one extra pair of holes 5 mm above and another 5 mm below. That’s three rows, two holes in each, 10 mm apart.
  2. Make the holes Ø 4 mm. Put a small square of tape over the foam first so the holes don’t tear. grown-up: bradawl
  3. Make gauge blocks 10, 15 and 20 mm tall from scrap wood, or folded card, and write the size on each.
  4. To change height: batteries out, stand the gauge on the bed, pull both pins on each side, rest the knife on the gauge, and push the pins into the row that lines up. Use the same row on both sides.
  5. Check the ramp and reel still clear everything, then do a first-spin test.

Upgrade 2 — Speed dial

A PWM speed controller is a little circuit board with a knob. It switches the power on and off hundreds of times a second. More “on” time means a faster motor. It lets you find the best knife speed by experiment, instead of buying different motors.

  1. Buy a small “PWM DC motor speed controller” board with a knob. Check the listing says it works from 6 V or less and handles at least 1 A.
  2. Batteries out. Disconnect the red wire between the switch and the button.
  3. Input: red wire from the switch to IN+; a new black wire from the battery’s black to IN−.
  4. Output: OUT+ to the hold-to-run button, and the button to the motor red. Motor black goes to OUT− instead of straight to the battery.
  5. Mount the board on the base plate with sticky pads, knob towards the handle. Keep it dry: no cress water near it.
  6. Experiment. Start with the knob at halfway. Cut a test strip, then try faster and slower. Write down the slowest setting that still gives a clean cut.
Why not just go slow?

Turning the knob down lowers the knife’s power as well as its speed, so at very slow settings a thick patch can stall it, and the fuse will trip. Fewer cuts a second also means you must push more slowly. Use the simulator to see why.

Upgrades 3 & 4 — Self-driving with automatic stop

Pushing at a perfectly steady speed is hard. A second, very slow N20 on the back axle drives the machine along at exactly the same speed every time. The automatic stop switch makes sure it can never drive off the end.

Upgrade 3 — Drive motor

  1. Buy a second N20, 6 V, 15 RPM, and 20 mm of silicone tube with a 3 mm hole.
  2. Work out the speed: wheel circumference = π × 30 mm ≈ 94 mm. 15 turns a minute × 94 mm = 1,410 mm a minute, or about 24 mm a second. With 10 knife cuts a second, that’s 2.4 mm between cuts — well within the golden rule.
  3. Lengthen the back axle so it sticks out 15 mm on one side. Push the silicone tube half onto the axle and half onto the motor shaft.
  4. Mount the motor on a foam-board block glued to the side plate, so the shaft lines up exactly with the axle. The tube soaks up small misalignment. grown-up: glue
  5. Wire it in parallel with the cutter motor: its red to the cutter motor’s red, its black to the cutter motor’s black — after the button. Add its own 100 nF capacitor.
  6. Check the direction. Press the button with the machine lifted: the wheels must turn so it would drive forward. If not, swap the drive motor’s two wires.

Upgrade 4 — End-of-row stop

  1. Buy a lever microswitch with three terminals: C, NO and NC.
  2. Wire C and NC into the red wire, between the ON/OFF switch and the hold-to-run button. Not NO! NC means “normally closed”: power flows until the lever is pressed.
  3. Mount it at the front of the chassis above one rail, lever pointing forward and down. Glue a wooden end block on that rail just past the end of the tray.
  4. Test it before any cress: hold the button so both motors run, then press the lever with a finger. Everything must stop. If not, re-check you used NC.
A real safety interlock

This is the same idea as the switch that stops a lift moving while its doors are open. The machine is safe even if someone forgets to let go of the button.

Upgrades 5 & 6 — Wider header and 3D-printed cutter

Upgrade 5 — Wider header

For a wider tray, everything across the machine gets longer by the same amount. Keep the tooth pitch at 10 mm and the stroke at 10 mm.

  1. Mind the load. Every extra tooth adds rubbing, so the fuse will trip sooner. Up to about 150 mm wide, the 300 RPM motor should cope; test by running with no crop and feeling if the motor gets warm.
  2. Wider than 150 mm? Either build two cutter units, one from each side, each with its own motor and knife meeting in the middle, or use a stronger 100 RPM N20 (about three times the turning force).
  3. With a 100 RPM motor there are only 3.3 cuts a second, so the golden rule limits you to 16 mm a second push speed.

Upgrade 6 — 3D-printed cutter bar

If there’s a 3D printer available (like the Ender-3 used for the PlaneForge aircraft), printed parts are straighter and more even than hand-cut plastic — and a better cut comes from perfectly spaced teeth.

  1. Design in Tinkercad (free, in a web browser): guard comb, knife, and a cutter body that includes the spacer and the hold-down lip in one piece.
  2. Sizes: knife 1.0 mm thick; slot for it 1.3 mm; guard 1.2 mm; same teeth as the templates.
  3. Crank with a D-hole: the N20 shaft is 3 mm with a flat at 2.5 mm. Print a test hole first, then adjust by 0.1 mm until it pushes on snugly without glue.
  4. Print flat on the bed in PETG (tougher and slipperier than PLA), 100% infill, 0.2 mm layers. Clean the first-layer “elephant’s foot” off the knife edges with fine sandpaper.
  5. Swap it in using the same motor, arm and split pin, then re-check the slide test.

Upgrade 7 — Conveyor-belt feeder

Real combines don’t rely on a slope — they use a conveyor (the feeder house) to carry the crop up. Replacing the ramp with a small moving belt means cress never piles up at the bottom.

What it is

A loop of 20 mm ribbon or a cut-open rubber glove cuff, running over two skewer rollers: one just behind the knife, one above the tank’s low front wall. Matchstick cleats glued across it every 15 mm grab the cress.

What drives it

A second small rubber band from the reel’s axle to the top roller. Like the reel, it only moves when the machine moves — so it needs no extra motor or wiring.

  1. Remove the ramp (a warm butter knife run under hot glue releases it). grown-up
  2. Rollers. Cut two 100 mm skewers. Wrap each with tape to 8 mm across in the middle, so the belt tracks centrally.
  3. Bearings. Glue short straws to the insides of the header arms: bottom roller just behind plate D, top roller above the tank lip. The belt should slope at about 30°.
  4. Belt. Loop the ribbon around both rollers and join the ends with a strip of tape on the outside, slightly tight. Glue on matchstick cleats every 15 mm.
  5. Drive. Make a tape pulley on the reel axle (outside the arm) and another on the top roller, and join them with a small rubber band.
  6. Direction check: push the machine forward by hand. The top of the belt must move up and back towards the tank. If not, cross the band.
Keep fingers clear

The conveyor has no motor of its own and stops when the machine stops, but rollers can still pinch. The batteries-out rule applies before clearing it.

The safety plan

For Mum: every risk we could think of, and what we do about each one.

RiskWhat we do about it
Craft-knife cutsA grown-up does all knife cutting on a mat with a steel ruler; the blade is retracted and put away straight after.
Hot-glue burnsLow-temperature glue gun, used by or right next to a grown-up; kept on its stand; bowl of cold water nearby.
Fingers in the cutterPlastic teeth, not metal; scissor action, not a spinning blade; hold-to-run button; guards stick out further than the knife; batteries out before hands go near the knife.
Electric shockNot possible: 6 V from AA batteries is far too low to be felt. No mains electricity anywhere in the project.
Battery short circuitBatteries live in a proper box; the fuse is the first thing after the battery; batteries out while wiring and when the machine is put away.
Motor jam or overheatingSelf-resetting 500 mA fuse; let go of the button if it stalls; the belt slips instead of breaking.
Small partsSplit pins, beads and caps kept in a closed box, away from younger children and pets.
Food hygieneWashed tray; clean hands; only candle wax as a lubricant; electrics above and behind the crop; cress rinsed before eating; any cress touched by glue thrown away.
Water and messTray on a board with a tea towel underneath; misting bottle instead of a jug; electronics kept dry.

Rules card (stick it on the handle)

  1. Batteries OUT before fingers go near the knife.
  2. Only press the button when the machine is on the rails.
  3. Both hands on the handle while it runs.
  4. Grown-ups do knives and hot glue.
  5. Smells hot or stops? Let go, switch OFF, batteries out.

Before every run — checklist

  • ☐ Knife slides freely, no cracked teeth
  • ☐ Crank pin has its stop on the tip
  • ☐ Wires tied clear of the crank and wheels
  • ☐ Button stops the knife the moment it’s released
  • ☐ End-stop switch tested (if fitted)
  • ☐ Tray on its board, towel underneath

Cheat sheet — so, Mum…?

  • What: a cress combine harvester, about 24 cm long, rolling on rails beside a shallow tray of home-grown cress.
  • How it cuts: a 6 V, 300 RPM N20 gear motor turns a crank; a pitman arm slides a plastic knife 10 mm over a plastic guard comb — 10 scissor cuts a second.
  • How it collects: a wheel-driven reel sweeps cress onto the knife; a gentle ramp leads into a dustpan-shaped tank.
  • The golden rule: move less than 5 mm between cuts — push at about 20 mm a second.
  • Safety: 6 V batteries, plastic knife, fuse → switch → hold-to-run button, grown-up does knives and glue, batteries out before touching the cutter.
  • Cost and time: about £20, two afternoons of building, one week of growing.
  • Upgrades: adjustable height, speed dial, self-driving with an automatic stop, wider header, 3D-printed cutter, conveyor feeder.
